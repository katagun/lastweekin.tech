    Number 4. AI The Register

    Watch out UK taxpayers: 28,000 HMRC staffers just got an AI copilot

    HMRC has deployed 28,000 Microsoft Copilot licences after a 20,000‑civil‑servant trial that showed an average 26‑minute daily time saving and high user enthusiasm, prompting the department’s chief AI officer to announce plans for “agentic‑style” features and a vision of becoming the world’s most AI‑enabled tax authority. The trial also highlighted limitations in handling complex, data‑heavy tasks and raised security concerns about sensitive data, raising doubts about using Copilot in official “Sensitive” workflows. HMRC frames Copilot as a bolt‑on to existing automation that has delivered £8 billion in benefits, but critics point to the government’s stale IT estate, duplicated content and the risk of embedding a generative system that is hard to turn off once adopted, leaving the agency with a tool that works well enough to rely on but is not yet trustworthy for critical decisions.

    Number 6. General Tech Ars Technica

    New robotic control software avoids jamming their joints

    EPFL researchers have created “Kinematic Intelligence,” a framework that lets robots switch between different arm models without retraining from scratch, mirroring how smartphones sync data when upgraded. The system addresses a long‑standing issue in robot learning‑from‑demonstration: skills learned on one robot often fail on another due to subtle differences in link lengths, joint orientations, or configurations. By automatically adapting learned behaviors to the new robot’s kinematic constraints, Kinematic Intelligence enables seamless transfer of tasks—such as wiping, stacking, or welding—across diverse robotic platforms.

    Number 7. General Tech WIRED

    Here’s How Much San Francisco Tech Companies Pay for Police Protection

    Elon Musk’s 2024 move of X’s offices outside San Francisco after calling the city’s violent crime “horrific” highlights a growing trend of tech firms paying for police protection under the city’s 10B rent‑a‑cop program, which allows businesses to hire officers at standard city rates. 2024 records show Airbnb spent about $428,000 on uniformed, armed officers, while Salesforce paid roughly $728,000 through a security vendor and added $41,000 for police at its TrailblazerDX conference. OpenAI and Anthropic have not been regular clients, though OpenAI briefly paid $813 for a museum event. The program, used by a range of companies—including the Giants baseball team ($1.9 M), Apple ($1.2 M), and various retailers—offers a low‑cost, quick‑response security solution, but raises concerns about conflicts of interest and liability, even as it provides officers with additional income and fosters community ties.
