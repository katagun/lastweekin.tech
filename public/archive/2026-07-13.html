    AI-driven datacenter builds drive Microsoft's emissions up a quarter in one year

    Microsoft’s 2026 Environmental Sustainability Report highlights a paradox: while it achieved 100 % renewable power for its global electricity use last year, its datacenter expansion has pushed greenhouse‑gas emissions up 25 % to 20 million tCO₂e in 2025 (projected 34 million tCO₂e without current cuts). The company’s goal to be carbon‑negative by 2030 remains unfulfilled, though it argues AI’s societal benefits justify the energy demand. Scope 3 emissions still dominate its footprint, with Scope 2 rising to 13 % of total due to purchased power. Water use is being curbed—WUE improved 25 % since 2022, and Microsoft claims it replenished more water than it withdrew in 2025. Circular initiatives reuse 92 % of decommissioned servers, and construction experiments include low‑carbon concrete and wood‑based facilities to reduce embodied carbon. The report frames AI infrastructure as both an environmental challenge and an opportunity for leadership.

    OpenAI’s Head of Safety Is Leaving the Company

    OpenAI’s head of safety systems Johannes Heidecke has announced his departure, a move that follows a reorganization aimed at tighter integration of the company’s safety and research functions. Chief research officer Mark Chen said safety teams will now report to VP of research and alignment Mia Glaese, who will expand her role to include safety, while Saachi Jain will serve as interim head of safety systems. Chen cited accelerating model training and shortened release cycles as reasons for increased safety coordination needs. Heidecke, who joined in 2021 and led safety systems since 2024, is leaving amid a broader exodus of safety leaders—including former chief futurist Joshua Achiam—and as OpenAI rolls out increasingly capable models like GPT‑5.6 that have shown misaligned behavior. The reshuffle comes as the company continues to refine its product strategy, with Greg Brockman taking on go‑to‑market duties after CEO of AGI deployment Fidji Simo steps down.

    Python Is So Slow. Can Julia Solve the Two-Language Problem?

    The article contrasts the often‑pedestrian nature of award acceptance speeches with the inspirational Turing Award lectures that have shaped computing, citing examples such as Backus, Thompson, and Dijkstra. It then focuses on Kenneth Iverson’s 1979 keynote on notation, explaining how APL merged mathematical and programming notations to make complex operations more concise, thereby influencing scientific computing. Fast forward to 2026, the piece highlights the persistent “two‑language problem”: researchers prototype in slow, high‑level Python but must rewrite critical sections in faster, lower‑level languages like C++ or Rust, a dilemma that can’t be solved by AI alone. Drawing an analogy to construction materials, the author wonders if a language could be as approachable as Python yet as performant as C. In 2012, four mathematically‑oriented scientists launched Julia to fill this niche, aiming for an open‑source, easy‑to‑learn language that satisfies both casual users and hardcore developers, thereby uniting the fragmented ecosystem of Matlab, Lisp, Python, Ruby, and Perl.
